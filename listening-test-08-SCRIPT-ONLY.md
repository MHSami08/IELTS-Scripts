## LISTENING PART 4

### Script: Part 4 (Questions 31–35)

```
Good morning, everyone, and welcome to today's lecture. I want to look at tidal energy — how it works, why it matters, and what some recent research tells us about its promise and its problems. As I mentioned last week, this is a timely topic, because several coastal nations are now investing heavily in it.

So, to begin with, how does tidal energy actually work? The basic idea is that modern tidal turbines are placed in fast-moving water, often at the mouth of a channel, and the movement of the water turns the blades. What is particularly noteworthy is that, unlike the wind, the tides never slacken, and, as a result, the output is highly reliable. That reliability is a real selling point.

However — and this brings us to the first obstacle — the engineering costs remain very high. Building concrete barrages and protecting turbines from salt water is expensive, and this has slowed the industry dramatically. Engineers also have to cope with extreme conditions, because a single site may experience two high tides every day, each moving an enormous volume of water.

Let me now turn to the research. Dr Elin Madsen and her team at the Nordic Ocean Institute have spent years measuring output at several working plants. She found that the average capacity factor was around ninety-four per cent, which is far higher than you would get with offshore wind. So on the question of reliability, her data are very encouraging.

Professor Tomasz Nowak, at the Baltic Technical University, has focused on the economics. He argues that until the costs of installation and upkeep fall, the technology will struggle to compete, and he predicts that prices will only ease once turbines are produced in larger numbers. On top of that, he warns that the rotating blades could injure passing seals and porpoises in the channels where the plants sit, which is an issue we cannot ignore.

Third, Dr Priya Raghavan of the Coastal Research Centre has examined the environmental side. Her team found that the blades can harm wildlife, and she also flagged the build-up of sediment behind river barrages, which slowly silts up the estuary upstream. Both effects, she argues, need careful management.

So, to sum up — the three researchers approach the same technology from different angles: Madsen shows it is reliable, Nowak warns it is costly, and Raghavan reminds us it must be done carefully. Next week we shall move on to policy responses.

It is helpful here to separate two very different ways of capturing the tide, because they are often confused. The first, tidal range, uses a barrier across an estuary, letting water pile up on one side and then drive turbines as it runs out. The second, tidal stream, simply parks underwater turbines in the current, like wind turbines laid on their sides. The range method delivers far more power but costs far more and alters the landscape; the stream method is gentler but yields less. Most of the recent development has concentrated on the second, precisely because it interferes less with ports and shipping.
```

### Script: Part 4 (Questions 36–40)

```
Now let me give you a little more detail on the technology and its history. The first modern tidal plant began operating in 1966, a small installation near the town of Saint-Malo in France. Since then, the industry has grown, though rather more slowly than many people had hoped.

In terms of design, the classic turbine looks rather like a windmill under the water, but a newer design rotates on a vertical axis, which allows the blades to turn slowly and more quietly. Some experts believe these vertical turbines will be the future of the industry.

I keep returning to the comparison with wind. In my view, the single greatest advantage of tidal energy over offshore wind is that it offers a more predictable supply, because the tides are governed by the moon rather than by the weather. That predictability is enormously valuable to grid operators.

So, where does that leave us? We have Dr Elin Madsen's figure of a capacity factor of around 94 per cent, which underscores the reliability I mentioned earlier. And we have Professor Nowak's warning that the rotating blades could disrupt migrating fish, as well as Dr Raghavan's notes on wildlife and sediment, which I will come back to in the next seminar. So the picture is genuinely mixed, and the challenge for engineers is to lower the capital outlay while keeping the environmental impact as small as possible.

The balancing act becomes clearer if we think about what the ideal site looks like. The best locations combine a strong, reliable current with deep, sheltered water, close to a grid connection and reasonably close to the towns that will use the power. That combination is rare, and it explains why so many promising projects live and die by detailed maps of the seabed. It also explains the appeal of pairing tidal schemes with batteries, so that the hours of peak generation can be shifted to the hours of peak demand. Seen that way, tidal energy stops being a curiosity and becomes part of a wider storage problem, which is, I think, the frame we should keep in mind.

To wrap up, tidal energy is reliable and clean, but it is neither cheap nor entirely without environmental cost. I would encourage you all to weigh those competing pressures yourselves, because the debate is far from settled. Thank you for your attention, and please do have a look at the reading list on the portal before next week.
```
