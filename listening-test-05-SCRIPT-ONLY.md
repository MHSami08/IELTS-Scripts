Right, good morning everyone, and welcome to the Sunward Energy Discovery Centre. I'm Fran, and I'll be showing you around this morning. The centre stands in what was originally a municipal power station, built in 1928 to light the city's northern districts. ## LISTENING PART 4

### Script: Part 4 (Questions 31–35)

```
Good morning, everyone. Today I want to talk about one of the great forgotten networks of the medieval world: the caravan trade that carried gold and salt across the Sahara desert. It is a topic where archaeology, written history and economic modelling have come together rather dramatically in the last twenty years, so I think you will find it rewarding — and it raises a genuinely difficult problem. I am going to do three things: first, describe how the trade actually worked; second, explain what it was worth; and third, look at why it eventually collapsed.

Let me start with the practicalities, because the caravans were astonishing operations. A really large caravan might include as many as two thousand camels, moving in a column several kilometres long, and everything the travellers needed — food, water, fodder — had to travel with it. The great north–south routes linked the salt-producing districts of the central Sahara with the farming regions of the river valleys far to the south.

Why was salt worth so much? In the desert climate, salt was essential for preserving food, and it also kept livestock healthy on the long marches. The kingdoms of the south had mined gold for centuries, and they were glad to exchange that gold for salt; this simple swap powered the entire system. The gold itself came from mines beside the upper Niger, far beyond the desert's edge, carried to the southern markets in refiner's baskets.

The crossing, of course, was slow and dangerous. A single journey between the salt diggings and the southern markets could last two months, with water rationed at every stage, and a caravan that missed a well usually came to grief.

And it is worth pausing on the scale implied by these routes, because the numbers are easy to understate. Rather than one caravan crossing in a straight line, the trade moved as a slow relay. Goods changed hands at a string of watering stations, so a load could take most of a season to cover what appears, on the map, to be a modest distance. Costs rose and fell with rainfall, because the wells that kept the animals alive were often only a few days of riding apart. That is why the merchants preferred to travel in large companies, and to rely on guides who knew every dry well by name.

The volumes were modest to begin with, but they climbed steadily. The Arabic accounts, which the historian Professor Idris Mansour has studied in the Cairo archives, describe how the trade grew steadily from the ninth century onwards, as Mediterranean courts developed an appetite for gold.
```

### Script: Part 4 (Questions 36–40)

```
Turning now to the value of the trade, and to three researchers who between them have transformed how we think about it. What is particularly noteworthy here is the sheer scale of the numbers involved.

Professor Idris Mansour, a historian at Cairo University, has spent twenty years working through the toll registers and merchants' letters preserved in the Cairo archives. His analysis shows that gold and slaves were carried northward year after year — the two great commodities of the desert routes — while salt and cloth travelled south on the return leg. He is also careful to remind us that the slave trade brings a dark reality into this story, and that we should not romanticise the caravans for a moment. His registers reveal, in addition, that copper bars circulated in the Saharan towns as a rough form of currency, exchanged alongside the silver arriving from the Mediterranean.

Dr Lena Vogel, an archaeologist at the University of Innsbruck, has worked on the northern salt diggings themselves. Her carbon-dating of packing materials has pushed the beginning of well-organised salt exports back to the tenth century, earlier than many textbooks claim, and her pottery sequences show that traffic rose steadily through the later medieval centuries. She points out that the desert was not a barrier but a corridor: the same wells and way-stations served the caravans for almost a thousand years.

And there is a final, surprising cargo. Manuscripts flowed across the desert too — works on law, astronomy and grammar travelled with the merchants — so that, for the scholars of the southern cities, the desert acted as a gateway to the wider Islamic world. This intellectual traffic barely survived the sixteenth century: once the Atlantic sea routes opened, both goods and ideas were redirected along the west African coast.

Dr Beatriz Solano, an economic historian at the University of Seville, has modelled the entire system using the shipping-house records of Lisbon and Seville. In summary, her work suggests the desert trade did not simply collapse — it was out-competed. Once it became cheaper to carry gold around the coast by sea, the caravans' advantage evaporated; she dates the collapse of the northward gold traffic to the early sixteenth century, though the salt trade, however, lingered longest, because salt had no sea-borne rival. So the real story of the desert routes, she concludes, is the story of how falling freight costs reshaped a whole continent.

The decisive shift came in the seventeenth century. Ships from the European ports began to supply gold and other prized goods directly to the coastal kingdoms, cutting the cost of the journey by sea rather than by sand. The sand routes simply could not match that speed or volume, and with each successful voyage the caravans lost a little more of their custom. By the eighteenth century the trade had shrunk to local and much humbler transactions. The great age of the trans-Saharan caravans was over, leaving a lasting trace only in the architecture and scholarship of the towns they had served.
```
