### Script: Part 2 (Questions 11–15)

```
Good morning, everyone, and welcome to the Caldmere Ironworks Museum. I'm Tomas, and I'll be guiding you around the site this morning. So, um, let me start with the story of the place.

The works were founded in 1814 by a local engineer, Elias Barlow, who devised a cheap method of casting railway parts. The foundry prospered and grew steadily, and at its height, around 1890, it employed over four hundred people. The business folded in 1972, largely because cheaper castings from overseas flooded the market, and the museum opened on the site three years later, in 1975.

Now, the furnace hall is the heart of the museum, and it still contains the original blast furnace from 1814, which we keep running during our demonstrations — it is quite something to see it fired up. To its side sits the engine house, which was added later, in 1860, when the foundry first turned from water power to steam.

Beyond the hall is the old water wheel beside the millpond. It no longer drives any of the original machinery, but the wheel still turns slowly and it now drives a small generator that lights the visitor centre. So, in a small way, it powers the building in front of you, which we think is a rather nice touch.

And if you have children with you, the hands-on corner usually steals the show. The youngsters love striking the anvil with cooling metal, under supervision, and they take home a small keepsake made in the workshop.
```

## LISTENING PART 4

### Script: Part 4 (Questions 31–35)

```
Good morning. Today's lecture concerns ice cores and what they reveal about the climate of the distant past. It is a field that has changed our view of natural climate variation, and it matters because it lets us set the present warming against what has gone before. I want to cover, first, how the cores are recovered and what they preserve; second, some of the leading findings from the drilling; and third, what the most recent layers tell us about the modern, factory-based era.

So, to begin with, the technique itself. Ice cores are obtained by drilling deep shafts down into the great ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland. As each year's snow layer is buried, it is gradually crushed into a single thin layer, and the layers stack up like the pages of a book, oldest at the bottom. What makes the cores so valuable is what gets sealed inside: tiny bubbles of air, trapped as the snow compacted, hold a genuine sample of the atmosphere from the season in which the snow first fell. The deeper a core reaches, the farther back in time it can look.

The most important reading a scientist takes is the ratio of heavy oxygen to light oxygen, because that balance acts as a natural thermometer. Water containing the lighter form evaporates more readily from the ocean, so the proportions frozen into the ice track the temperature of the era with great fidelity. The elegance of the method lies in its simplicity, for a single isotopic measurement acts as a proxy for a quantity as intangible as past warmth. Dust offers a second line of evidence. A high concentration of wind-blown dust in a layer indicates that conditions were windier, and, tellingly, the glacial intervals of the past were markedly stormier than our own temperate age.

Here I can mention one specific and influential study. Dr Ingrid Halvorsen, working at the Nordic Polar Institute, combined carbon dioxide measured from the bubbles with the oxygen-temperature record stretching back eight hundred thousand years. She established that a decline in carbon dioxide accompanied every major cooling, whereas a rise in the gas accompanied each brief warming, and the correlation she found is remarkably tight, which leaves us confident that the link between the gas and the cold periods is a real one rather than chance.
```

### Script: Part 4 (Questions 36–40)

```
Let me turn now to what the recent layers add to the picture. Professor Wei Lin, at a Chinese earth-sciences school, measured soot and heavy metals in the ice of the past three centuries, and he found that the sharpest jump came after 1950, which he ties closely to the postwar manufacturing surge across Asia and Europe. The timing is so consistent across the cores that it is now treated as a reliable marker of that very surge.

Beyond these pollutants, the ice also records the warming gases themselves. The ice holds both carbon dioxide and methane directly, so a researcher can compare a past reading with the modern record, and the two agree closely wherever the layers overlap. That agreement is what makes the longer projections reliable, because it confirms the method rather than relying on assumption. One might reasonably ask whether such agreement is coincidental, and yet it has now been reproduced across dozens of sites, which substantially strengthens the credibility of the underlying reconstructions.

Now, temperature and gas are not the only signals locked in the ice, because the physical structure of the layers carries further clues. A few teams have tried to deduce past snowfall from the thickness of individual layers, although the signal is faint and the interpretation is disputed. One of those researchers, Dr Samira Okafor, a Kenyan scientist, has used thickness records to argue that dry spells in the tropics were considerably longer in earlier centuries, a claim that is still being tested against other evidence. Were that hypothesis ultimately corroborated, it would arguably require older reconstructions of tropical rainfall to be treated with somewhat reduced confidence.

Finally, and most practically, the cores act as an independent check on modern instruments. The readings they give for the recent period are constantly weighed against those from the monitoring stations at the surface, and where the two match, they refine our estimates of how the climate will respond in the coming decades. Beyond the three main lines of evidence I have described, the cores also clarify the scale of natural variation, which is essential for distinguishing the human contribution from background oscillation. The interpretation nevertheless requires caution, because subtle disturbance during drilling, or even the slow migration of gas within the ice, can introduce measurable error, and the community treats reconstructions as probabilities rather than certainties. So, in summary, ice cores tie the present to the deep past and they sharpen the projections that planners rely upon, because a firmer understanding of past change, which these records uniquely provide, is what gives the models their more secure footing. Such reliance, however carefully the discipline proceeds, is likely to deepen as ever longer cores are recovered in the decades ahead. Next week we will look at how these records are combined with tree rings and ocean sediment to build an even fuller picture of the changing climate.

And that cross-check matters more than it may sound, because every line of climate history is only as trustworthy as the instruments that recorded it. Where the two records conflict, it is the ice that usually wins.
```
