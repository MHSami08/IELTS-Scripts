```
Good morning, Brookfield, and welcome to the mid-morning programme. I'm Annie Voss. Now, I want to talk this morning about the Milestone Ridge Walking Festival, which takes place this Sunday. It's organised by the Willowside Footpath Trust, and it seems to grow a little more every single year.

First, a little background, because the festival has an interesting history. It began fifteen years ago, but not for the reason you might guess. The old railway had just been pulled up, and the council organised that first walk to celebrate the reopening of the high lane as a public footpath. We should be clear that it started as a celebration, full stop. Over the years the trust has added smaller charity walks to its calendar each spring to raise money for local causes, and the new footbridge over the creek arrived two years later, but the reopening of the footpath remains the founding moment, and it stays at the heart of the event.

Now, the practical information for this Sunday. In the early years we used the village hall, and there's still a help desk in the market square, but that is purely for last-minute queries. Registration on the day takes place at the old station house, just behind the trust's gazebo on the old platforms. So head straight for the station house.

The route has changed this year. The walk now swings south along the river for a much longer stretch, so the full loop works out at about eleven miles. In past years the shortest version stayed up on the ridge all the way and only reached the water right at the end. There's also a four-mile loop for a shorter day out, but the main walk is the long one.

And the prices. Adults pay six pounds for the day, and children take part free of charge. A family group, that's two adults and up to three children, pays ten pounds for a family ticket. So the family option is ten pounds, which beats buying separately.

Two small practical notes before I go. Do carry a trail map, because phone signal is weak along the ridge. You can leave the packed lunch at home, since the farm stalls at the halfway point serve hot food all afternoon. And although a waterproof is handy, dressing in layers is the stronger advice this time of year. I'll run through the route details and timings after this message.
```

```
Dr Naylor: Let me point you toward some studies for the literature review, because you'll want real research behind you. Dr Petra Lindqvist has argued that raw frequency is less decisive than researchers once assumed — the sheer number of encounters does not fix the memory as firmly as people suppose.

Sam: Yes, that's a good one. And Professor Jonas Weber found that encountering a word in two different contexts beats seeing it ten times in the same book. That supports our reading idea directly.

Leila: I remember Dr Amara Diallo's earlier work too — she showed that learners retain words better when they meet them in varied sentences. So her finding sits right next to Weber's.

Sam: Right. And Dr Tunde Bakare took a more cautious line — her experiments suggested that guessing a word from context rarely leads to lasting recall, which is the caution we need for the write-up.

Dr Naylor: Yes, we should report that honestly. Dr Elise Fontaine, meanwhile, found that reading a word aloud strengthens its memory trace, which is a handy practical tip for learners.

Leila: And Dr Marco Voss warned that sounding out new words slows fluent reading, so the advice has to be balanced.

Sam: That's a strong set — support and a caution together.
```

## LISTENING PART 4

### Script: Part 4 (Questions 31–36)

```
Good morning, everyone. Today's lecture concerns the life beneath our feet, and the difference that tiny organisms in the soil may make to the way we farm. It is a subject in which biology, crop science and practical land management meet, and one that, arguably, has attracted growing attention from researchers in recent years, because the evidence now points in a direction that few anticipated. I want to do three things this morning: first, describe what these organisms typically do; second, look at three researchers whose work suggests how farming choices may shape the underground community; and finally, consider what growers might, in principle, take from their findings.

So, to begin with, some sense of scale. The organisms living in the ground are astonishingly abundant, although they account for only a sliver of the soil's total mass. One gram of fertile soil can hold more than a billion of them, packed into every crumb. What is particularly noteworthy is that their numbers are not the whole story: they drive the system. They continually recycle the nutrients that plants require, and in doing so they help build soil structure, the network of aggregates that lets air and water move through the ground. Indeed, remove them, and that structure slowly collapses, which is why the preservation of this community may ultimately matter more than its sheer size.

Let me turn to the first researcher. Dr Helena Marsh, a microbiologist at the University of Lantor, has spent more than a decade tracking these underground communities in working fields, an exercise that presumably constitutes a substantial body of evidence. Her central finding concerns the fine threads that spread through the soil and connect plant roots. Where the ground is worked aggressively, she found, those threads are damaged deeply; where the land is left to rest, they flourish. Just as important is the exchange at the heart of the relationship: the threads trade directly with the roots, passing the plant phosphorus in return for sugars the leaves produce. Ploughing, Marsh concludes, severs that trade, and repeated disturbance appears to prevent its re-establishment almost indefinitely.

The second body of work comes from Professor Tomas Rivera, an agronomist at the Cordoba Valley Institute, who studies legumes and whose trials, it must be said, typically span entire growing seasons. His field-scale trials demonstrated that legume crops, through the bacteria inside their root nodules, add nitrogen to the soil, and that a well-planned rotation therefore removes much of the need for synthetic fertiliser, which is, potentially, the most consequential economic outcome. Over three seasons Rivera measured wheat yields fifteen per cent higher on rotations that kept legumes in the system, and, crucially, the advantage grew as the soil community rebuilt itself.

The third strand of research is Dr Aisha Bello's, at the Ederra Research Station. She has spent several winters watching bare fields wash away, convinced that the loss is, arguably, the most underestimated part of the damage. Where the ground was left bare, heavy rain stripped away the topsoil at a worrying rate; where a winter crop shielded the surface, she recorded a fall in runoff of forty per cent. The message, she argues, is that the soil is best defended by being fed, since a well-managed field tends to conserve its deepest layers of its own accord.
```

### Script: Part 4 (Questions 37–40)

```
Let me now bring these three lines of work together, and say something about what they mean in practice, because the practical implications are, in my view, considerable. Taken together, they point to a simple conclusion: the health of the farm depends on the health of the soil, and above all on the fungi that run the underground trade. Marsh's networks, remember, deliver phosphorus to plant roots; Rivera's rotations work because nitrogen is fixed by partners buried in the ground; and Bello's winter fields hold their moisture because the soil keeps its structure. So when we talk about the microbial life of farmland, we are, in a real sense, talking about the fungi, the living threads that stitch the whole system together, which is a point worth emphasising.

What follows for growers? First, that there is real value in cover crops. Bello's results suggest that keeping the land clothed over winter both protects the topsoil and feeds the underground community, and that the benefit compounds, so to speak, year after year. Rivera makes the same point from the other side: bare fields sitting between cash crops are an open wound, although the consequences may take years to become fully apparent.

Yet there is a caution running through all of this. The very treatments that protect yields can poison the soil's inhabitants, and the phenomenon is, evidently, far more widespread than official guidance once acknowledged. Marsh's later data show that routine applications of fungicide reduce the same networks her restoration work tries to rebuild; the build-up of fungicide in intensively managed plots, she warns, is a genuine concern.

So, to sum up, these three researchers approach the subject from different directions, and their message converges, which is itself a remarkable outcome. The microbes — the bacteria and fungi we rarely see — are the silent foundation of sustainable farming. Feed the microorganisms beneath your feet, and the reward appears in stronger yields, cleaner water and more resilient land, though the economics of the transition remain, on the whole, contested. Next week, we will examine the subsidy questions that follow.

Looking ahead, the question of how to pay for these changes is the one that provokes the liveliest disagreement among economists, and it is to that debate that we will turn in the final lecture of the term.
```
