Good morning, everyone, and welcome to Kingswood Botanic Garden. I'm Lena, and I'll be taking you round the main collections this morning. So, um, let me start with something of the history.

The glasshouse dates from the 1870s, and it still holds its original iron frame. It is worth stressing that the glass itself is modern — the old panes were replaced in the 1990s — but the frame is genuinely Victorian. By contrast, the café beside it was built in the 1920s, in a very different style.

Now, the rose terrace. You may think of roses as delicate, but here we grow old-fashioned types, the ones our grandparents knew, with strong scents. We stopped growing climbing varieties against that famous pergola years ago, because they had become too tangled to manage. And do note that the summer concerts are held in the bandstand, not on the terrace — the terrace is far too narrow for the audience.

The herb garden was created in 2004, mainly to solve a quiet problem. The fruit trees were not setting enough fruit, and the staff discovered the reason was a shortage of pollinating insects. So the herbs were planted deliberately to attract bees and butterflies. We do pick some herbs for the café kitchen, but that is a happy side effect rather than the original aim. And the cooking classes are held in the education studio by the gate, not in the garden.

For the lake loop, it is not the ten-minute shortcut some visitors assume, and it does not eat up a full afternoon the way the long woodland route does. The loop around the lake takes about half an hour at a gentle stroll, and it passes the reed bed.

If you have children with you, the willow dome is usually their favourite — they love climbing inside it. I should add that we ask visitors not to feed the koi in the lake, as the fish are on a strict diet, and the miniature train only runs at weekends, I'm afraid.

## LISTENING PART 4

### Script: Part 4 (Questions 31–35)

```
Good morning. Today I want to talk about deep-sea hydrothermal vents — the extraordinary hot springs that dot the ocean floor — and about the strange communities of life that cling to them. I'll do three things: first, how these vents form; second, what lives around them; and third, why they have come to matter to mining companies and the risks that creates.

To begin with, how does a vent form? Cold seawater seeps down through cracks in the seafloor and is heated by contact with volcanic rock far below. The heated water then races upward again, carrying a rich load of dissolved minerals that it has drawn out of the surrounding rock. When this superheated fluid meets the near-freezing water at the seafloor, the minerals drop out of solution and pile up, and over many years they build tall chimneys — some reaching the height of a small tower. Because the vents appear wherever the great plates of the seafloor pull apart, these structures are found in long lines along the mid-ocean ridges.

The chemistry here is astonishing in its extremes. The fluid pouring out is scorching — Professor Lars Brandt, at a Scandinavian oceanography institute, has logged vent temperatures that exceed three hundred and fifty degrees Celsius, while the water a few metres away is barely above freezing. Although the vent water is dark and bitter, it teems with life, and it is the microbes that anchor the whole food web. With no sunlight reaching this depth, the bacteria turn the dissolved chemicals, above all hydrogen sulphide, into usable energy — a process biochemists call chemosynthesis. Dr Hana Okoye, working at a Pacific marine institute, has shown that the giant tube worms clustering on the chimneys have no mouth or stomach at all; they rely entirely on the bacteria that live inside them for their food. So here the food chain begins in the dark, powered by chemistry rather than light.

Around those chimneys live crabs, shrimp, and blind fish, each one adapted to the crushing pressure and the darkness. The vent fields support levels of life far richer than the barren mud of the surrounding plain, precisely because they carry energy in chemical form. In places, whole hillsides of clam shells show how long these oases have persisted, and they remind us that entire food webs can rise from the seafloor without ever seeing the sun.

And a word about scale, because the biology here is stranger than it first appears. The creatures that cluster around a single vent can form a dense community within a few hundred metres of the opening, yet they are almost entirely dependent on the flow of heated water. When that flow weakens, the whole gathering shifts or dies within months. What looks like a permanent habitat is in fact a passing settlement, and this mobility is one of the reasons the systems are so hard to survey. Even a carefully planned study may be recording a community that has already moved on.
```

### Script: Part 4 (Questions 36–40)

```
Now this is where the story takes a worrying turn. The very minerals that build the chimneys — copper, zinc, and silver — are the ores that mining companies now hunt for on land. As surface deposits are exhausted, deep-sea mining has moved to the front of the industry's agenda, and the richest ore fields lie precisely around the vents. Dr Mei Tanaka, at an East Asian deep-sea centre, estimates that a single mature vent field can hold an amount of copper comparable to a good-sized land mine, and that has made the sites intensely attractive.

The danger is that dredging or drilling a vent would crush the chimneys and destroy the compact ecosystem they support in a matter of hours. Several oceanographic institutions have therefore proposed protecting a network of the best-known vents, setting them aside as marine reserves where nothing may be extracted. Yet a deep obstacle remains. The rules that govern mining on the deep-sea floor are shared between nations, and no single government can act alone; the main barrier to protecting the sites is the lack of binding international agreement. The states that hold mining licences are reluctant to give up ground, while conservationists demand verification that is hard to arrange in the dark miles below. On land, a country can simply pass a law; at sea, consent must be won from many parties at once, and that negotiation moves slowly.

And there is a further reason to hesitate before the first machines are lowered. The technology that would carve ore from the seabed is still young, and the costs of repairing what it damages would fall on everyone who depends on the health of the ocean. The cautious position, the one the research community increasingly shares, is that exploration privileges should be granted slowly and revoked as soon as they are unused, so that no activity ever gains a right simply by being first.

So, to sum up: hydrothermal vents are fragile, ancient, and full of life we barely understand. We can learn enormously by stationing research vessels above them and by leaving a portion of the ridge untouched. Professor Brandt's temperature records and Dr Okoye's biology together show how much remains undiscovered, and the deeper concern is that the mining may outrun the science. There is also a question of timing, because the number of licensed exploration blocks grows each year even as the scientific picture stays incomplete. The consensus among marine geologists now favours a shared moratorium across nations until we can agree how best to protect these extraordinary deep-sea gardens. Next week we will turn to how such an agreement might be written, and to the costs of enforcing it.
```
